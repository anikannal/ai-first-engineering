Assessment Rubrics

Three assessments across the program. Each tests a different dimension of AI-first engineering competence. No written exams.

Assessment 1: Code Walkthrough

Format: 10–15 min, 1:1, human coach
When: End of Phase 1 (W5), Phase 2 (W11), Phase 3 (W16)

Assessor preparation

Before the walkthrough, review the student's GitHub activity for the phase and the Course Coordinator's pre-brief. Select 2–3 code sections: - At least one AI-generated (visible in PR descriptions) - At least one connecting to other parts of the system - Vary across the squad

Dimension 1: Comprehension

1
Cannot explain the code's purpose. Describes syntax ("it's a function, it has parameters") without meaning.
2
Explains the general purpose but misses key details or gets some behaviour wrong.
3
Accurately explains inputs, outputs, and side effects. May need a prompt or two.
4
Explains fluently and precisely, including edge cases and system fit, without prompting.

Dimension 2: Critical Evaluation

1
Accepts code as-is. Cannot identify any potential issues even when prompted.
2
Identifies one obvious issue when explicitly asked, but can't explain why it matters.
3
Proactively identifies at least one issue or improvement and explains the reasoning.
4
Identifies multiple issues, reasons about tradeoffs, suggests a concrete path forward.

Dimension 3: AI Awareness

1
Cannot distinguish AI-generated from human-written sections. Has not thought about this.
2
Aware AI generated some code but can't explain what they verified, changed, or decided to keep.
3
Can articulate what AI generated, what they changed, what they verified, and why they trusted the output.
4
Demonstrates sophisticated judgment: knows when to accept, modify, or reject AI output and explains the heuristics developed.

Dimension 4: System Thinking

1
Can only discuss code in isolation. Cannot reason about its place in the system.
2
Vague sense of the code's place in the system but cannot trace dependencies clearly.
3
Can explain how this code connects to adjacent parts and what would break if it changed.
4
Reasons fluently about system role, identifies implicit dependencies, considers downstream effects.

Scoring

Score Outcome
13–16 Distinction
10–12 Pass
7–9 Borderline — coaching conversation required
4–6 Does not meet standard — remediation required

Note: A score of 1 on any dimension, regardless of total, triggers a mandatory coaching conversation.

Assessor question bank

Comprehension: "Walk me through what this function does." / "What happens if this input is null?" / "Why is this structured this way?"

Critical evaluation: "Is there anything here you'd change?" / "What edge cases does this miss?" / "If this was AI-generated, what would you verify before shipping?"

AI awareness: "Did you write this, or did the AI?" / "What did you change from what the AI generated?" / "How did you decide to trust this output?"

System thinking: "What would break if this function was removed?" / "Where does this data come from?" / "What happens downstream if this returns an error?"

Assessment 2: PR History Review

Format: Async review + 15-min feedback conversation
When: End of Phase 2 (W11) and Phase 3 (W16)

Review both PRs authored and PRs reviewed across the phase.

Dimension 1: PR Communication

1
PR descriptions absent or minimal ("fixed stuff"). No context given.
2
Descriptions focus on what changed technically, not why.
3
PRs explain purpose, reference the relevant task, note what reviewer should check.
4
PRs are exemplary: context, tradeoffs considered, AI sections flagged explicitly.

Dimension 2: Commit Discipline

1
Commits are infrequent, enormous, or have no meaningful messages.
2
Commits are inconsistently sized and messaged. Hard to follow.
3
Reasonably atomic commits with clear messages describing what changed.
4
Commit history reads as a clear narrative. Anyone could follow the development from commits alone.

Dimension 3: AI Collaboration Transparency

1
No evidence AI's role was considered. AI-generated sections undifferentiated.
2
Occasionally notes AI involvement but inconsistently, without reflection.
3
Consistently notes AI contributions, explains what was verified or modified.
4
Treats AI as a collaborator to be cited. PR descriptions include clear attribution and critical judgment about AI output quality.

Dimension 4: Review Quality

1
Reviews absent or approvals only.
2
Superficial comments (typos, formatting) — no substantive issues raised.
3
At least one substantive comment per PR: real issue, clarifying question, or meaningful improvement.
4
Thorough reviews that add clear value. AI-generated code scrutinised explicitly. Reasoning explained.

Assessment 3: Portfolio Artifact

Format: Written (1,500–2,500 words), video (8–12 min), or presentation (15 slides + notes)
When: Submitted W15, prior to Demo Day

Dimension 1: Honesty and Depth of Reflection

1
Reads as a product description. No real reflection on process or AI use.
2
Generic reflection ("AI helped me code faster"). No specific examples.
3
Specific, honest reflection on both successes and failures. Reader learns something real about how this student worked.
4
Exceptionally honest and insightful. Non-obvious lessons, genuine mistakes acknowledged, mature relationship with AI as a tool.

Dimension 2: Technical Credibility

1
Technical content vague or incorrect. Could have been written without building anything.
2
Technical content present but superficial. Describes what was built without demonstrating understanding.
3
Accurate and appropriately detailed. The reader can tell this student knows what they built.
4
Precise and confident. Architectural decisions and tradeoffs explained. Goes beyond describing code to reasoning about it.

Dimension 3: Specificity of AI Examples

1
AI examples absent or entirely generic.
2
Some specific examples but without enough context to be instructive.
3
Clear, specific examples with enough context (task, prompt, output, changes) that a reader could learn from them.
4
Exemplary case studies. Actual prompts included, output evaluated, evaluation process described. Genuinely instructive.

Dimension 4: Communication Quality

1
Poorly structured, hard to follow. Not appropriate to share with an employer.
2
Readable but unpolished. Technical terminology inconsistent.
3
Well-structured, clearly written, appropriate for a technical audience. Would represent the student well.
4
Distinctive and compelling. Student's voice comes through. Would stand out in any hiring context.

Portfolio scoring

Score Outcome
13–16 Distinction
10–12 Pass
7–9 Borderline — revision opportunity offered
4–6 Does not meet standard — full revision required
